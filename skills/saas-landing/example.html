Why we rewrote our sync engine in Rust

By Mira Hassan · April 22, 2026 · 8 min read

For two years our Go sync engine was good enough. Then video editors joined the customer list, and GC pauses we'd been ignoring became bug reports we couldn't ignore.

The decision wasn't sudden. We'd watched P99 latency creep for six months before admitting what the data said. Customers syncing 30 GB of project files in active sessions were the ones writing in.

Rewriting an entire sync engine sounds like the project startups are told never to do. We did it anyway.

The trigger: GC pauses we couldn't fix

Go's garbage collector is brilliant — and a tradeoff. Our hot path allocated short-lived buffers on every block diff. At scale, P99 pause crept past 50ms. Video teams needed under 5.

We can't fix this in Go. We can fix it in something without a GC.

What we kept; what we threw out

The CLI and control plane stayed in Go. The hot-loop diffing path became Rust behind a single FFI surface.

Every cross-language boundary is an external API the day you ship it.

Results

  • P99 sync latency: 38ms → 4ms
  • Memory on heavy uploaders: down 62%
  • RFC to production: 11 weeks

What I'd do differently

I'd generate FFI bindings from a schema instead of hand-rolling cgo wrappers. Two production incidents taught us that lesson.
